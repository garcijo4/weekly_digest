## Scoop risk

**Study 3 (anchors vs. suitability factors in robo-advice).** Two new papers sit in Study 3's domain — AI-generated financial recommendations and what fraction of that content moves into a person's actual choices — without doing Study 3's specific design.

1. **Choi, Kim, Kovach, Lee, Shin & Tzavellas, "Do People Follow AI Advice? Evidence from a Pension Portfolio Choice Experiment"** (arXiv:2608.11371, econ.GN, submitted Aug 11, 2026). Verified abstract (paraphrase from the fetched arXiv page): 400 South Korean workplace-pension participants allocated hypothetical funds across eleven investment products, then could revise after seeing an AI-generated recommendation in a 2×2 design (aggressive/conservative recommendation × with/without rationale). ~37% of the experimentally-induced gap between aggressive and conservative recommendations passed through into final portfolios; 81% of participants revised, and of those, 95% moved toward the AI's suggestion while implementing roughly half the suggested adjustment.
   — Threatens the general empirical territory of Study 3 (AI numeric/directional recommendations pulling client portfolio choices) but stops well short of the actual design. It measures human pass-through of a *directional* recommendation (aggressive vs. conservative), not an uninformative numeric anchor, and does not attempt the exchange-rate framing (anchor value expressed in years of investment horizon) or any suitability-factor tradeoff. What remains fully unclaimed: the anchor-vs-suitability exchange rate itself, any Reg BI / FINRA 2111 framing, and any test using an anchor known ex ante to carry zero information. If anything this strengthens the case for Study 3 — it shows the pass-through channel is real and large (81%/95%) — rather than scooping it. Worth citing in Study 3's related work.

2. **Choukhmane, de Silva, Lin & Akuzawa, "AI Financial Advice: Supply, Demand, and Life Cycle Implications"** (NBER Working Paper No. 35574, posted August 2026; exact posting day not shown by NBER or RePEc). Verified abstract (paraphrase from the fetched NBER page, cross-checked against the authors' own posted copy — near-verbatim match): respondents write prompts seeking spending/investing advice from LLMs; the authors simulate lifetime effects of following that advice (GPT-5.2) under realistic asset- and labor-market conditions. Following the advice moves respondents toward life-cycle-consistent behavior: broader participation in diversified equity funds, age-declining equity shares, larger savings buffers. Recommendations vary by gender, prior AI experience, and financial literacy; for gender specifically, two-thirds of the recommended equity-share gap comes from men and women writing different prompts (demand), one-third from gender labels attached to otherwise identical prompts (supply).
   — Adjacent to Study 3's domain but no anchor, no exchange rate, no suitability-factor tradeoff — not a design threat. Mainly relevant as evidence that "LLM financial advice" is now an NBER-level research area, and as theoretical support discussed below.

## Bears on your research

- **NBER WP 35574's normative-pull finding supports the Normative-Descriptive Divergence mechanism.** The result that following LLM financial advice pushes recipients toward life-cycle-*normative* behavior (broader diversification, age-declining equity share, bigger buffers) is independent, cross-domain evidence for the theory behind "Homo Silicus is Hyper-Rational": that GPT-class outputs regress toward the prescriptive/normative benchmark embedded in training corpora rather than reproducing typical human behavior. This is advice-giving behavior, not the model's own trading decisions, so it is not a replication of the disposition-effect or attention-shock claims — but it is a second domain in which the "corpus overweights prescriptive text" story generalizes, and worth a citation in the theory section.
- **The paper's supply/demand decomposition is a useful methodological template.** Isolating "prompt content written by different users" (demand) from "model's own response to an identity label on an otherwise-identical prompt" (supply) is a clean way to separate persona-label effects from user-behavior effects. It bears on open question 1 (do personas bind LLM behavior, or are they defeasible instruction sets?): here, label-driven (supply-side) sensitivity accounts for only about a third of the observed gender gap, with user-driven prompt content accounting for the rest — suggestive that, in this financial-advice setting, persona-label effects are real but are the smaller component next to what the human actually asks. Worth considering this supply/demand split as a robustness check for any of Garcia's own persona-prompt designs.
- **arXiv:2608.11371 bears on the deployment/regulatory strand** (LLMs as financial advisors, delegation, suitability). It is a human-subject study, not an LLM-as-subject study, but it demonstrates that AI-generated recommendations achieve high real-world pass-through (81% revision rate, 95% directional compliance) in an actual retirement-portfolio context. That is the human-reception side of the channel "Algorithmic Anchoring" studies from the LLM-output side (contamination propagating into buy/sell recommendations) — worth citing as evidence for why contamination propagating through an LLM advisor has real downstream stakes.

## Evidence on the eight open questions

**Q1 (do personas bind LLM economic behavior, or are they defeasible instruction sets overridden by other signals?).** NBER WP 35574's supply/demand decomposition (see above) is indirect but relevant evidence: model-side response to a gender label (supply) explains roughly a third of the observed gender gap in AI-recommended equity shares, with user-driven prompt-content differences (demand) explaining the rest — a data point toward "persona/identity-label effects are real but modest relative to prompt content" in the financial-advice domain specifically.
