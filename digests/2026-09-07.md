## Bottom line
No scoop risk to any of the five planned studies this week — SSRN and NBER were both genuinely quiet after exhaustive sweeps, and nothing on arXiv or in the journals gets close enough to any planned design to flag as a threat. The one item worth reading closely is arXiv:2609.03218 ("The Analyst in the Prompt"), a 12-model, 3,575-filing test showing persona/role framing distorts *interpretation* of identical financial evidence and that two plausible mitigations only partially fix it — the closest infrastructure yet to Study 3's premise, though it doesn't touch anchoring or suitability factors directly. A second arXiv paper (2609.02580) finds LLM double-auction markets converge to equilibrium *more slowly* than human markets, a market-level inefficiency result that sits in interesting tension with, but doesn't contradict, the individual-level hyper-rationality claim in "Homo Silicus." ## Bears on your research

- **arXiv:2609.03218, "The Analyst in the Prompt: Role, Retrieval, and Memory Biases in LLM Financial Analysis"** (Ahmed Asaad, Amr Mohamed, Yang Zhang, Omneya Abdelsalam; submitted 2026-09-02; cs.CL, preprint). Verified via arXiv abstract page (paraphrase): tests 3,575 SEC filings across 12 LLMs to decompose user-context effects on financial analysis into "evidence selection" (what gets retrieved) versus "evidence interpretation" (how identical evidence is read) under persona-conditioned versus neutral retrieval and memory-framed context. Most of the context-driven spillover comes from interpretation of identical evidence under different roles/personas, not from retrieving different evidence. Two mitigations — framing the investor mindset as a user profile rather than an assistant role, and separating evidence-based from personalized output — reduce but do not eliminate the spillover, with effectiveness varying across models. **Bears on open question 1** (do personas bind or are they defeasible?) with a large, financial-domain-specific answer: persona/role framing measurably and non-trivially distorts interpretation of identical evidence, and known mitigations only partially work. This is also consistent with — and indirectly supports — the disembodiment conjecture behind *Algorithmic Anchoring*: this is a language-statistical, role-conditioning effect (not affect-mediated), and it transfers robustly across 12 models. It is the closest thing found this week to Study 3's premise (that suitability/persona-style framing produces real, hard-to-eliminate bias in LLM financial output) but does not construct an anchor, a suitability factor, or an exchange-rate framing — no scoop, but worth citing as supporting evidence for the design's core assumption.

- **arXiv:2609.02580, "Competitive Market Behavior of LLMs"** (Łukasz Struski, Marcin Świstak, Anna Okulska, Przemysław Biecek; submitted 2026-09-02; cs.MA, preprint). Verified via arXiv abstract page (paraphrase): replicates classic double-auction market experiments with LLM agents; markets populated by LLM agents show slower or no convergence to equilibrium and less efficient allocations than human-populated markets, with significant behavioral divergence across model families and market roles. Chain-of-thought analysis shows a shift from strategic reasoning toward urgency-driven behavior. **Bears on open question 7** (do LLM agent markets avoid or produce bubbles and mispricing?) with a market-level inefficiency finding, and is worth noting as a complicating data point alongside — not a contradiction of — "Homo Silicus is Hyper-Rational": that paper's hyper-rationality claim is about individual response to attention shocks and disposition, not market-level equilibrium convergence, so an individual agent could still track normative prescriptions while a market of heterogeneous model families fails to coordinate on efficient prices. Worth a citation flagging the distinction between individual-level normative conformity and market-level efficiency as two separable claims that don't automatically move together.

- **arXiv:2609.02797, "Dutch Books for Language Models"** (Isaiah Andrews, Suproteem Sarkar; submitted 2026-09-02; econ.GN, cross-listed cs.AI/cs.CL/cs.LG, preprint). Verified via arXiv abstract page (paraphrase): elicits probabilistic forecasts from LLMs over events generated from stock-returns data and computes Dutch-book arbitrage profit via linear programming against the models' stated probabilities (a de Finetti coherence test); finds substantial probabilistic incoherence that worsens with logically complex event structures, and that irrelevant contextual information amplifies the incoherence. **Bears on standing interest 2** (prompt-embedded numerical cues and how contamination propagates) and standing interest 5 (statistical inference on model-generated data) — a name-brand econometrics co-author (Andrews) using stock-return-generated events specifically, with a clean result that irrelevant context degrades coherence. Not an anchoring study and not a scoop on Study 1, but a useful contemporaneous methods citation for the general claim that context a model shouldn't be using still moves its outputs.

- **arXiv:2608.30522, "Tariff Threats, Macroeconomic Expectations, and Policy Communication Strategies: Experiments Based on a Multi-Agent System"** (Jianhao Lin, Lexuan Sun, Yixin Yan; submitted 2026-08-31; econ.GN, preprint). Verified via arXiv abstract page (paraphrase): converts 300 Michigan Survey households into persistent LLM agents exposed to simulated social-media information streams over several months; calibrated agents reproduce real survey expectation patterns following tariff announcements; immediacy, rate salience, semantic progression, message complexity, narrative framing, and sender identity jointly shape inflation and unemployment expectations, attributed to attention, credibility, and narrative-interpretation mechanisms; central-bank messaging can coordinate beliefs across agents. **Bears on standing interest 3** (attention, salience, and social signals as drivers of simulated behavior) and **open question 4** (do framing effects transfer?) — a macro-expectations rather than trading-decision domain, but a clean demonstration that salience/narrative framing measurably moves LLM-agent beliefs, and that calibrated personas reproduce real survey patterns (bears on open question 1 from the "persona carries real content" side).

- **Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Finance, "Rationality and financial risk aversion in large language models: Evidence from a multi-model Holt–Laury experiment"** (Danny de Castro Soares, Lucas Eduardo Barbosa de Freitas Bittencourt, Mathias Schneid Tessmann; DOI 10.1016/j.jbef.2026.101235; peer-reviewed). Confirmed via Crossref (title, authors, venue, DOI) and cross-checked via Semantic Scholar and Unpaywall (listed as CC-BY hybrid open access). **Abstract text could not be retrieved** — ScienceDirect returned HTTP 403 on both the paywalled abstract page and the nominally-open-access PDF, and Semantic Scholar's abstract field was empty. The title implies a multi-model Holt-Laury price-list elicitation of LLM risk aversion, which would bear directly on standing interest 1 (do documented human biases reproduce, attenuate, or invert?) and the risk-preference-elicitation strand of standing interest 5 — but per verification discipline, **findings beyond the title are not verified** and should not be cited until full text is accessed (recommend checking via institutional subscription). Note on timing: Crossref records the article as first available online 2026-08-13 (before this week's window) and only received its formal September-issue date stamp this week — it was not on a prior digest's already-known list, so it is reported here as newly surfaced, with that caveat made explicit.

## Evidence on the eight open questions

**Q1 (do personas bind LLM economic behavior, or are they defeasible?).** arXiv:2609.03218 adds a large-scale, financial-domain data point on the "binds" side: persona/role framing measurably distorts interpretation of identical evidence across 12 models, and known mitigations only partially remove it. arXiv:2608.30522 separately shows calibrated household personas reproduce real survey expectation patterns, consistent with personas carrying real behavioral content rather than being purely defeasible instructions.

**Q4 (do framing effects transfer?).** arXiv:2608.30522: salience, narrative framing, message complexity, and sender identity jointly and measurably shape LLM-agent macroeconomic expectations — a macro rather than micro-finance domain, but positive evidence for framing-effect transfer.

**Q7 (do LLM agent markets avoid or produce bubbles and mispricing?).** arXiv:2609.02580: LLM-populated double-auction markets converge to equilibrium more slowly, and produce less efficient allocations, than human-populated markets, with significant divergence across model families.

No new evidence found this week on Q2 (capability-vs-normative-benchmark monotonicity), Q3 (affect-mediated bias transfer), Q5 (causes of hyper-rationality), Q6 (direction of the disposition effect), or Q8 (cross-model/cross-draft reproducibility) beyond the incidental multi-model heterogeneity noted in 2609.02580 and 2609.03218, which doesn't rise to a direct reproducibility finding.
